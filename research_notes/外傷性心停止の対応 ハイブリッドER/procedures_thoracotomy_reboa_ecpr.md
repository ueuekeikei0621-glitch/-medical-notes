## 1. EAST practice management guideline on emergency department thoracotomy (EDT) [2015] and any update

### Takeaway
EAST 2015 (GRADE, 72 studies, 10,238 EDT patients) gives one **strong recommendation FOR** EDT: pulseless with signs of life (SOL) after penetrating thoracic injury. It gives **conditional FOR** recommendations for: penetrating thoracic without SOL; penetrating extrathoracic with or without SOL; blunt with SOL. It gives a **conditional AGAINST** recommendation for blunt injury without SOL. I found no adult EAST update through Nov 2026. The only EAST-linked update is a 2023 pediatric guideline (PTS/WTA/EAST), and the WTA published a separate adult algorithm in 2024 (section 2).

### Cited Findings
- **Citation:** Seamon MJ, Haut ER, Van Arendonk K, et al. "An evidence-based approach to patient selection for emergency department thoracotomy: A practice management guideline from the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma." J Trauma Acute Care Surg 2015;79:159-173 [2015]. — [PubMed 26091330](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26091330/)
- **Method and evidence level:** GRADE systematic review. Meta-analysis was "not possible because no comparison or control group (i.e., survival or neurologically intact survival data for similar patients who did not undergo EDT) was available". Level of evidence is "Systematic review/guideline, level III". In other words, every recommendation rests on uncontrolled case series. — [PubMed 26091330](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26091330/)
- **Pooled EDT outcomes by group (EAST 2015)** — [PubMed 26091330](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26091330/):
  - Penetrating thoracic, SOL present: survival 182/853 (21.3%); neurologically intact survival 53/454 (11.7%)
  - Penetrating thoracic, SOL absent: survival 76/920 (8.3%); neurologically intact 25/641 (3.9%)
  - Penetrating extrathoracic, SOL present: survival 25/160 (15.6%); neurologically intact 14/85 (16.5%)
  - Penetrating extrathoracic, SOL absent: survival 4/139 (2.9%); neurologically intact 3/60 (5.0%)
  - Blunt, SOL present: survival 21/454 (4.6%); neurologically intact 7/298 (2.4%)
  - Blunt, SOL absent: survival 7/995 (0.7%); neurologically intact 1/825 (0.1%)
- **Recommendations, verbatim:** "We strongly recommend that patients who present pulseless with signs of life after penetrating thoracic injury undergo EDT. We conditionally recommend EDT for patients who present pulseless and have absent signs of life after penetrating thoracic injury, present or absent signs of life after penetrating extrathoracic injury, or present signs of life after blunt injury. Lastly, we conditionally recommend against EDT for pulseless patients without signs of life after blunt injury." — [PubMed 26091330](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26091330/)
- **EAST definition of signs of life,** as restated in a 2024 study citing the guideline: "Signs of life include pupillary response, spontaneous ventilation, presence of carotid pulse, measurable or palpable blood pressure, extremity movement, or cardiac electrical activity." — [Godbole et al., Cureus 2024 (PMC11026983)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11026983/)
- **EAST vs WTA in practice:** a single urban level I center, 2012–2019, 87 RTs.
  - "WTA guidelines were met in 78/87 (89.7%) of cases, comparatively EAST guidelines were met in every case."
  - In 9 cases (10.3%) the indications were discordant (EAST conditional, no WTA indication), "due to the protracted duration of CPR before proceeding to RT".
  - Overall RT survival was 6/87 (6.9%); all 6 survivors met both sets of criteria.
  - Single-center retrospective, low quality. — [Godbole et al., Cureus 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11026983/)
- **Pediatric update [2023] (outside adult scope, listed for completeness):** PTS/WTA/EAST GRADE guideline. It makes a "strongly recommend against EDT in the patient without SOL after blunt injury" recommendation and a conditional FOR recommendation for pulseless children with SOL after penetrating thoracic or abdominopelvic injury, or after blunt injury "if emergency adjuncts point to a thoracic source". Quality of evidence was "very low". — [Selesner et al., J Trauma Acute Care Surg 2023 (PubMed 37608453)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37608453/)
- **Counterpoint commentary:** Hatchimonji & Seamon, "Emergency Department Thoracotomy in Penetrating Chest Trauma Patients with No Signs of Life: A Worthwhile Endeavor" (World J Surg 2023). Read as title only; no abstract available. — [PubMed 37833545](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37833545/)

### Inferences
- The EAST framework turns on **mechanism × SOL at ED arrival**. It sets no CPR-duration cutoff, so it is more permissive than WTA. In the Godbole series, every WTA-eligible case was EAST-eligible, but not the reverse.
- For a resident, the only "must do" (strong) indication is **penetrating chest + pulseless + SOL**. The only "lean against" indication is **blunt + no SOL**, with about 0.1% neurologically intact survival.
- "Conditional" here reflects very low certainty. The numerators are tiny and selection-biased, because only patients chosen for EDT were reported.

### Gaps
- I found no adult EAST EDT guideline update dated 2016–2026 (Europe PMC title search). If EAST has a guideline in development, it was not findable.
- The verbatim SOL definition comes from a secondary paper quoting EAST. The EAST full text was not accessible to confirm the wording.

## 2. Western Trauma Association (WTA) resuscitative thoracotomy algorithm [2012] and its 2024 update

### Takeaway
WTA uses **CPR-duration gates**. In 2012 these were: blunt <10 min prehospital CPR; penetrating torso <15 min; penetrating neck/extremity <5 min; or profound refractory shock. Asystole without tamponade means the patient is declared dead. Salvageability is SBP >70 mmHg (with cross-clamp if needed). The **2024 update** (Tesoriero et al.) keeps the blunt <10 / penetrating <15 min gate. It adds explicit **ultrasound nodes** ("Cardiac motion on US?", "Tamponade?") before ERT or termination, a "Profound Shock (SBP <60)" pathway, and "Consider Zone 1 REBOA" as an alternative for abdominopelvic sources.

### Cited Findings
- **Evidence base [2011]:** prospective WTA multicenter study, 18 institutions, 2003–2009, 56 survivors.
  - "there were survivors of blunt torso injuries with CPR up to 9 minutes and penetrating torso wounds up to 15 minutes."
  - "Asystole was documented at ED arrival in seven patients (12%); all these patients had pericardial tamponade and three (43%) had good functional neurologic recovery".
  - Futility criteria: RT "can be considered futile care when (a) prehospital CPR exceeds 10 minutes after blunt trauma without a response, (b) prehospital CPR exceeds 15 minutes after penetrating trauma without a response, and (c) asystole is the presenting rhythm and there is no pericardial tamponade." — [Moore et al., J Trauma 2011 (PubMed 21307731)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21307731/)
- **WTA 2012 algorithm [2012]:** Burlew CC, Moore EE, et al., J Trauma Acute Care Surg 2012;73:1359-63. — [PubMed 23188227](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23188227/)
  - Indications: "blunt trauma patients with less than 10 minutes of prehospital CPR, penetrating torso trauma patients with less than 15 minutes of CPR, patients with penetrating trauma to the neck or extremity with less than 5 minutes of prehospital CPR, and patients in profound refractory shock."
  - After RT: "patients in asystole without cardiac tamponade are declared dead. Patients with a cardiac wound, tamponade, and associated asystole are aggressively treated."
  - Salvageability: "the patient's ability to generate a systolic blood pressure of greater than 70 mm Hg with an aortic cross-clamp if necessary."
  - Outcome estimates: success "approximates 35% for the patient arriving in shock with a penetrating cardiac wound and 15% for all patients with penetrating wounds", versus "2% survival for patients in shock and less than 1% survival for patients with no vital signs" in blunt trauma.
  - Applicability: "may not be applicable at all hospitals caring for the injured".
  - Evidence: no RCTs; observational data plus expert opinion.
- **WTA 2024 update [2024]:** Tesoriero R, Coimbra R, Biffl WL, et al. "Adult emergency resuscitative thoracotomy: A Western Trauma Association clinical decisions algorithm." J Trauma Acute Care Surg 2024 (Dec), DOI 10.1097/TA.0000000000004462. No abstract; full text paywalled. — [PubMed 39451159](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39451159/)
- **Official WTA 2024 flowchart nodes** (text extracted from WTA PDF) — [WTA Adult ERT Algorithm PDF](https://www.westerntrauma.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ERT-Algorithm-Procedures.pdf):
  - Entry: "Prehospital arrest and/or arrives pulseless with CPR in Progress?" → "Continue CPR, Airway/Oxygenation, Access, Transfusion, Thoracostomies, External Hemorrhage Control" → "Signs of Life (SOL) on arrival?" → "Only cardiac electrical activity/PEA?"
  - Time gate: "Blunt trauma with CPR < 10 minutes, penetrating trauma with CPR < 15 minutes" → "Cardiac motion on US?" → "Tamponade?" → "Emergency Resuscitative Thoracotomy (go to ERT procedure guide)" or "Terminate resuscitation and pronounce dead".
  - Non-arrest pathway: "Profound Shock?" (note 3: "SBP < 60 mmHg") → airway, access, transfusion, thoracostomies, external hemorrhage control, pelvic binder → "Improvement / ROSC?"
  - If no improvement → "Assess Mechanism and Injury Pattern":
    - "Penetrating or Blunt Thoracic Injury" → ERT
    - "Penetrating or Blunt Abdominal/Pelvic Injury" → "Tamponade?" → no → "Consider Zone 1 REBOA" (alternative branch: "REBOA Unavailable or ERT preferred")
    - "Penetrating or Blunt Head, Neck / Extremity Injury" → "Continue resuscitation, external hemorrhage control, and adjuncts", "Consider ERT or REBOA".
  - Note 1: "interruptions in CPR to facilitate resuscitation procedures are accceptable [sic] … Do not delay ERT in injury patterns likely to benefit."
  - Note 2, SOL: "Pupillary response, spontaneous ventilation, presence of pulse or measurable blood pressure, extremity movement, electrical cardiac activity."
  - Note 4, ROSC: "Organized cardiac rythym [sic] and SBP > 70 mmHg".
  - Note 5: "At surgeon descretion [sic] and where available REBOA may be considered as an alternative for aortic occlusion when no concern for thoracic aortic injury".
- **WTA 2024 ERT procedure guide (technique-level decision points)** — [WTA Adult ERT Algorithm PDF](https://www.westerntrauma.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ERT-Algorithm-Procedures.pdf):
  - "Left anterolateral thoracotomy & right tube thoracostomy". Note: "May consider initial bilateral anterolateral clamshell thoracotomy to facilitate exposure".
  - "Large left hemothorax?" → "Evacuate hemothorax, control hemorrhage, clamp hilum if necessary".
  - "Open pericardium and assess heart" → "Tamponade and/or cardiac injury?" → "Repair or temporary control of injury" → "Move to OR if ROSC".
  - "Organized cardiac activity?" → "SBP < 70" → "Clamp descending aorta and continue resuscitation".
  - "Large HTX or active bleeding from right chest?" → "Clamshell Thoracotomy".
  - If no organized activity → "Concern for air embolism?" → "Control site of air entry / Hilar clamp, Trendelenberg [sic], Aspirate Heart".
  - Note 3: "If no cardiac activity may consider a brief period of open cardiac massage, ATLS resuscitation, and aortic occlusion to improve coronary circulation". If still no organized activity → "Terminate resuscitation efforts".
- **Institutional protocol built on WTA 2024 [Nov 2024]** (UAB) — [UAB ERT Guideline Nov 2024](https://www.uab.edu/medicine/surgery/images/Trauma_and_Acute_Care_Surgery/Protocols/Emergent_Resuscitative_Thoracotomy_011325.pdf):
  - Summary of WTA 2024: "Cardiac motion on ultrasound supports proceeding with ERT"; "Absence of cardiac motion suggests cessation of resuscitation"; "Integration of REBOA (if available and appropriate) may complement ERT."
  - Technique: internal shocks "at 20 Joules"; pericardium opened "at least one finger breadth anterior to the phrenic nerve"; cross-clamp "just above the diaphragm", using an OG/NG tube to tell aorta from esophagus.
  - Sequence mnemonic "A-A-B-B-C-C": Airway, Access, Bilateral Chest Tubes, Blood Products, Cardiac US, CPR.
  - Before stopping in non-ERT candidates: "both pleural spaces decompressed to rule out other potentially reversible causes".

### Inferences
- **2012 → 2024 changes**, as far as the flowchart shows:
  - Ultrasound (cardiac motion, tamponade) is now an explicit gate in the CPR-in-progress pathway.
  - Thoracostomies are bundled into initial resuscitation.
  - REBOA is a named alternative for infradiaphragmatic sources, at surgeon discretion.
  - The 2012 "<5 min CPR for penetrating neck/extremity" criterion does not appear as a separate time gate in the extracted 2024 flowchart text. Neck/extremity injuries are routed to "Consider ERT or REBOA" within the shock pathway. Verify this against the full paper.
- **Who decides:** the algorithm is written for trauma surgeons at trauma centers ("At surgeon discretion"). In an ED where the emergency physician starts the resuscitation, the operational implication is early surgeon activation and pre-agreed local criteria.
- The flowchart arrows were reconstructed from text extracted from the PDF layout. Check the exact branch topology (e.g., what follows "SOL present but not PEA-only") against the image or the paper.

### Gaps
- I could not read the full WTA 2024 text: its rationale, literature synthesis, and whether it formally retires the 5-minute neck/extremity criterion.
- The flowchart does not say whether time limits refer to prehospital CPR only or to total CPR. The 2012 version says "prehospital CPR".

## 3. Ultrasound (POCUS) in TCA decision-making: cardiac motion, pericardial effusion, futility

### Takeaway
Absent cardiac motion on POCUS strongly predicts death: no survivors in a 2021 meta-analysis, and zero survival when both motion and pericardial fluid are absent (Inaba 2015). Pericardial fluid (tamponade) remains a "do not stop" finding even in asystole. WTA 2024 builds both into its gate. The SHoC meta-analysis says numbers are too small to justify termination based solely on POCUS.

### Cited Findings
- **Inaba et al. [2015], prospective, 187 traumatic arrests undergoing RT with FAST** — [Inaba et al., Ann Surg 2015 (PubMed 26258320)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26258320/):
  - 51.3% penetrating. "Sustained cardiac activity was regained in 48.1%. However, overall survival was only 3.2%" (plus 1.6% organ donors).
  - "Cardiac motion on FAST was 100% sensitive and 73.7% specific for the identification of survivors and organ donors."
  - "The likelihood of survival if pericardial fluid and cardiac motion were both absent was zero."
- **Cureton et al. [2012], retrospective, 162 pulseless trauma patients with EKG + ultrasound** — [Cureton et al., J Trauma Acute Care Surg 2012 (PubMed 22743379)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22743379/):
  - With EKG activity: survival to admission 23.5% with cardiac motion vs 1.9% without.
  - Without EKG activity: 66.7% vs 0%.
  - Cardiac motion predicting survival to admission: sensitivity 86%, specificity 91%, PPV 30%, "negative predictive value, 99%".
  - Sensitivity was 100% in penetrating trauma (n=111) but only 75% in blunt (n=50).
- **SHoC meta-analysis [2021], 8 studies, 710 TCA cases** — [Lalande et al., Resuscitation 2021 (PubMed 34437998)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34437998/):
  - "No patient with cardiac standstill survived."
  - Cardiac standstill predicting failure to achieve ROSC: sensitivity 91%.
  - Heterogeneity was substantial.
  - "The numbers of patients included in published studies remains too low for practice recommendations for termination of resuscitation based solely upon the absence of cardiac activity on POCUS."
- **Asystole with tamponade can survive:** all 7 ED-asystole survivors had tamponade; 3 had good neurologic recovery. — [Moore et al., J Trauma 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21307731/)
- **Use in guidelines:** WTA 2024 nodes "Cardiac motion on US?" → no → "Tamponade?" → no → "Terminate resuscitation and pronounce dead". — [WTA 2024 flowchart](https://www.westerntrauma.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ERT-Algorithm-Procedures.pdf)
- **Other prognostic factors (meta-analysis):** Tran et al. (2020; 53 studies, 37,528 patients) identified cardiac motion on ultrasound, initial shockable rhythm, witnessed arrest and bystander CPR as associated with higher survival. This was taken from a search summary; I did not open the primary article. — [search-result reference to Tran 2020 via PMC10591838](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10591838)

### Inferences
- Practical reading: **no cardiac motion + no pericardial fluid → strong futility signal**, especially in blunt TCA beyond the time windows. **Pericardial fluid → treat as tamponade** (thoracotomy), whatever the rhythm.
- Sensitivity is lower in blunt trauma (75% in Cureton), and a few studies report rare survivors without motion. POCUS therefore supports termination decisions together with mechanism, time and SOL; it should not be used alone.
- Inaba's specificity of 73.7% means cardiac motion does not predict survival. Most patients with motion still die.

### Gaps
- The Tran 2020 meta-analysis was not opened directly (only cited via search summary), so its pooled estimates are not reported here.
- I found no data on how hemothorax or subcutaneous emphysema affects FAST adequacy (Inaba reported 3.7% inadequate).

## 4. REBOA: indications, contraindications, evidence in TCA, society positions, REBOA vs RT

### Takeaway
REBOA treats **infradiaphragmatic** non-compressible hemorrhage. Zone 1 is for abdominal/retroperitoneal bleeding; Zone 3 is for pelvic/junctional bleeding. It is **contraindicated with major thoracic hemorrhage or tamponade**. The only RCT (UK-REBOA 2023; mostly blunt, not a TCA trial) showed no benefit and probable harm. The four-society US statement (2019) says REBOA "does not confer any long-term survival advantage when used in traumatic cardiac arrest". AORTA-registry data show similar or poorer TCA outcomes for REBOA versus RT, with longer time to occlusion. The JTDB propensity analysis (Japan) found higher mortality with REBOA. WTA 2024 allows REBOA as an aortic-occlusion alternative at surgeon discretion when there is no concern for thoracic aortic injury.

### Cited Findings
- **US joint statement [2019]** (ACS COT, ACEP, NAEMSP, NAEMT; with US military representation; updates the 2018 ACS COT/ACEP statement) — [Bulger et al., Trauma Surg Acute Care Open 2019 (PMC6802990)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6802990/):
  - Zones:
    - "Inflation in the distal thoracic aorta (Zone 1) is used for control of severe intra-abdominal or retroperitoneal hemorrhage."
    - "Inflation in the distal abdominal aorta (Zone 3) is used for patients with severe isolated pelvic, junctional, or proximal lower extremity hemorrhage not amenable to a tourniquet."
  - TCA and contraindications: "REBOA does not confer any long-term survival advantage when used in traumatic cardiac arrest compared with standard of care." "REBOA is contraindicated in the setting of major thoracic hemorrhage or pericardial tamponade."
  - Bias: "Comparing REBOA to resuscitative thoracotomy (RT) introduces both survival bias and bias by indication, as the patients undergoing RT are almost uniformly in cardiac arrest, thereby favoring REBOA."
  - Time limits:
    - "Zone 1 REBOA should not be used if patients cannot proceed expeditiously to a definitive hemorrhage control procedure within 15 min."
    - "Total aortic occlusion times greater than 30 min are associated with increased ischemic complications and risk of mortality."
    - Zone 3: "target less than 30 min, but no greater than 60 min of total occlusion time."
    - "Systems which cannot initiate definitive hemorrhage control within the above recommended times should not use REBOA."
  - Who places it: "REBOA should only be placed by a surgeon or interventionalist responsible for definitive hemorrhage control or by a physician trained and qualified in REBOA in direct consultation with the physician who will provide definitive hemorrhage control."
  - Prehospital: only "in the extremely rare circumstance in which a physician experienced in REBOA placement is on scene" and the system can meet "<15 min Zone 1, <30 min Zone 3".
  - Evidence (per page fetch): "The quality of clinical evidence to support REBOA use in trauma patients is poor with no Class I or II data".
- **The earlier 2018 statement [2018]** (ACS COT + ACEP) drew criticism from military authors as too prescriptive. The 2019 revision included the military. — [Brenner et al. 2018 (PMC5887776)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5887776/); [DuBose et al. letter 2018 (PMC5887830)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5887830/); [Qasim et al., Ann Emerg Med 2018 "Warrants Revision" (PubMed 30031514)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30031514/)
- **UK-REBOA RCT [2023]:** Jansen JO et al., JAMA 2023;330:1862-1871. Pragmatic Bayesian RCT at 16 UK major trauma centers, Oct 2017–Mar 2022, patients ≥16 y with exsanguinating hemorrhage. — [PubMed 37824132](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37824132/); [PMC10570916](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10570916/)
  - Primary outcome, 90-day mortality: "25 of 46 patients (54%) … vs 18 of 43 patients (42%)", OR 1.58 (95% credible interval 0.72–3.52), "posterior probability of an OR >1 … 86.9%".
  - Secondary mortality outcomes: ORs increased "with earlier mortality end points".
  - Deaths due to bleeding: 8/25 (32%) vs 3/18 (17%), mostly within 24 h.
  - Conclusion: REBOA "does not reduce, and may increase, mortality".
  - Trial details, from the full text:
    - Only about 3% penetrating injuries.
    - REBOA inserted and inflated in only 19/46 (41%); arterial access failed in 8 (17%).
    - Zone I in 10/19, Zone III in 9/19.
    - Median ED arrival-to-inflation time 32 min (IQR 20–47).
    - Stopped early for harm.
    - 23% needed CPR on ED arrival.
    - The main text has no explicit TCA inclusion/exclusion rule; exclusions were pregnancy and injuries "clearly not survivable".
- **AORTA registry/single-center data in arrest:**
  - Maryland, 2013–2017: 50 REBOAs for traumatic arrest, all Zone 1. 30-day mortality in arrest was 90%. ROSC occurred in 58%, and 38% of those survived to the OR. "All patients who survived AR gained full neurologic recovery." — [Brenner et al., JAMA Surg 2018 (PubMed 28973104)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28973104/)
  - Planned secondary analysis of a prospective 6-center study (2017–2018): 72 TCA patients (26 REBOA, 46 RT). Mortality 88% vs 93% (p=0.767). Time to aortic occlusion was longer with REBOA (7 vs 4 min). Adjusted RR 0.89 (0.71–1.12). Level III. — [Koh et al., J Trauma Acute Care Surg 2023 (PubMed 37381147)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37381147/)
  - AORTA 2013–2022, 1,937 patients: REBOA Zone 1 in 25.9% vs RT 74.1%. REBOA patients were more often blunt (76.3% vs 37.7%) and less often had prehospital CPR (23.2% vs 49.8%). REBOA use was associated with SBP ≥60 and GCS >8; RT with penetrating trauma. In other words, these are "different tools for different patients" and populations are not comparable. — [Coccolini et al., Injury 2025 (PubMed 40701854)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40701854/)
  - AORTA 2017–2023, excluding penetrating chest injury, 921 patients (83.1% died): partial REBOA associated with lower mortality than complete REBOA (aHR 1.84) and EDT (aHR 3.32). The abstract's reported CIs (1.01–1.60; 1.96–2.78) do not contain the point estimates, which looks like an internal inconsistency in the abstract; interpret with caution. — [Dewey et al., Transfusion 2025 (PubMed 40059692)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40059692/)
  - In patients with TBI (AORTA, 282 patients, 55% with ongoing CPR at occlusion): "the odds of mortality are 3.1 times higher for RT compared with REBOA". Level 4. — [Brenner et al., TSACO 2022 (PMC8928364)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8928364/)
- **Japan (JTDB, DIRECT-IABO):**
  - Propensity-matched JTDB (625 vs 625 torso-trauma patients): in-hospital mortality 61.8% with REBOA vs 45.3% without (+16.5%, 95% CI 10.9–22.0). The instrumental-variable sensitivity analysis gave +16.4% (−0.6 to 33.3). Level III. — [Inoue et al., J Trauma Acute Care Surg 2016 (PubMed 26808039)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26808039/)
  - DIRECT-IABO registry (2011–2015): 106 trauma REBOA cases. 28% of trauma patients also required RT. 24-h mortality was 51% and hospital mortality 64%. — [Matsumura et al., Eur J Trauma Emerg Surg 2018 (PubMed 28831501)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28831501/)
- **Prehospital REBOA (systematic review, 2026):** studies at "critical to serious risk of bias". Survival to discharge was 44% (14/32) in civilian trauma cases; the data are too small and heterogeneous to draw conclusions. — [Sun et al., Prehosp Emerg Care 2026 (PubMed 42530919)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42530919/)
- **Single-center retrospective, 2012–2022:** EDT mortality 90.1% vs REBOA 58.8% (strongly confounded by indication). The authors say REBOA is "an appropriate salvage maneuver" for abdominopelvic hemorrhage but its indications should not be liberalized. — [Spence et al., Cureus 2025 (PMC11973399)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11973399/)

### Inferences
- **REBOA vs RT for aortic occlusion in TCA:**
  - Thoracotomy is the only option that also treats tamponade, intrathoracic bleeding and air embolism, and allows open massage.
  - REBOA occludes only and is contraindicated in exactly the lesions that most often cause salvageable TCA (penetrating cardiac/thoracic).
  - REBOA fits best in **blunt, infradiaphragmatic (abdominal/pelvic) hemorrhage, in impending arrest or profound shock (SBP <60) rather than established arrest**, with a surgeon or IR ready for definitive hemorrhage control within about 15 min (Zone 1). This is the WTA 2024 "abdominal/pelvic, no tamponade → consider Zone 1 REBOA" branch.
- In a hybrid ER, the 2019 statement's time targets (occlusion → definitive control <15 min for Zone 1) are more achievable. That is an inference, not tested evidence. UK-REBOA's harm signal was linked to delayed or failed hemorrhage control (median 32 min to inflation).
- All non-RCT REBOA–RT comparisons carry confounding by indication; the 2019 statement explicitly warns of this.

### Gaps
- **JETEC (Japan Endovascular Trauma Course)** and Japanese society position statements on REBOA and RT were not retrieved here. They are presumably covered by the Japanese-guideline workstream.
- I found no RCT of REBOA specifically in TCA. ERC 2025 text on REBOA could not be accessed (HTTP 403).
- No dedicated EAST REBOA practice-management guideline was found by title search.

## 5. Pericardiocentesis vs thoracotomy for tamponade in TCA; bilateral finger/simple thoracostomy

### Takeaway
For traumatic tamponade causing arrest, **thoracotomy (pericardiotomy) is the definitive procedure**. Survival from tamponade-TCA is the highest of any TCA subgroup (21% in the London prehospital RT series) but strongly time-dependent, with no survivors beyond 15 min. Pericardiocentesis has largely been displaced in trauma and is now a temporizing or bridge option where thoracotomy is not immediately possible. Bilateral thoracostomy (finger/simple) to exclude tension pneumothorax is part of the initial TCA bundle in WTA 2024 and in physician-led prehospital systems. Outcome data for thoracostomy are weak.

### Cited Findings
- **Pericardiocentesis (PCC) systematic review [2013; pre-2015 but the key synthesis]** — [Lee et al., J Trauma Acute Care Surg 2013 (PubMed 24064864)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24064864/):
  - Review of 27 studies, 2,094 trauma patients with suspected tamponade.
  - PCC use fell from 45.9% (1970s) to 6.4% (2000–2010); use as the sole intervention fell from 13.7% to 2.1%.
  - Reported survival after PCC was 83.4%, a highly selected, non-arrest-dominated population.
  - "PCC is now offered as an optional skill in the ninth edition of the ATLS®".
- **NTDB 2011–2022 [2025], 1,036 trauma pericardiocentesis patients:** 59% blunt. Median time to PCC was 38 min for penetrating and 78 min for blunt trauma. 79% of penetrating vs 34% of blunt patients went to the OR afterwards. The proportion of penetrating PCC patients with cardiac injuries declined from 55% to 27%. — [Katsura et al., Am J Emerg Med 2025 (PubMed 40907275)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40907275/)
- **Tamponade TCA treated with RT (London HEMS, 1999–2019, 601 prehospital RTs):** "22 of 105 patients (21%) with cardiac tamponade" survived to discharge, vs 1.9% with exsanguination. "There were no survivors beyond 15 minutes of TCA for cardiac tamponade and 5 minutes after exsanguination." Retrospective. — [Perkins et al., JAMA Surg 2025 (PMC11866073)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11866073/)
- **London technique sequence:** "(1) bilateral finger thoracostomies in the fourth intercostal space; (2) if no immediate return of spontaneous circulation" → proceed to thoracotomy (clamshell). — [Perkins et al., JAMA Surg 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11866073/)
- **ERC 2021 [2021]:** per a search-engine snippet, the TCA algorithm states "Perform resuscitative thoracotomy or ultrasound guided pericardiocentesis" for tamponade. I could not open the full text (HTTP 403) to verify the wording or context. — [ERC 2021 Special Circumstances, Resuscitation 2021](https://www.resuscitationjournal.com/article/S0300-9572(21)00064-2/fulltext) ([PubMed 33773826](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33773826/))
- **REBOA and tamponade:** REBOA "is contraindicated in the setting of major thoracic hemorrhage or pericardial tamponade". — [2019 joint statement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6802990/)
- **WTA 2024 and tamponade:** tamponade on ultrasound → ERT. Inside ERT: "Tamponade and/or cardiac injury?" → "Repair or temporary control of injury". UAB options for temporary control: digital pressure, "skin stapler or Foley catheter". — [WTA 2024 flowchart](https://www.westerntrauma.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ERT-Algorithm-Procedures.pdf); [UAB protocol 2024](https://www.uab.edu/medicine/surgery/images/Trauma_and_Acute_Care_Surgery/Protocols/Emergent_Resuscitative_Thoracotomy_011325.pdf)
- **Thoracostomy in WTA 2024:** "Thoracostomies" are part of the initial bundle for every CPR-in-progress patient. The ERT itself pairs a "Left anterolateral thoracotomy & right tube thoracostomy". — [WTA 2024 flowchart](https://www.westerntrauma.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ERT-Algorithm-Procedures.pdf)
- **Lausanne physician-led prehospital service, 2012–2024 (bilateral thoracostomy protocolised since 2012):**
  - Of 247 TCA resuscitations, thoracostomy was judged indicated in 223 (90%) and performed in 148 (66%).
  - 27 (18%) of those with thoracostomy were alive on hospital arrival; 9 (6.1%) at 48 h.
  - "Larger studies are required to determine the impact of thoracostomy on survival."
  - Retrospective, single-center. — [Beysard et al., Resusc Plus 2025 (PMC11795094)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11795094/)
- **BestBET [2017]:** "Finger thoracostomy appears to be an acceptable and effective technique for trained physicians in the pre-hospital setting." Based on three papers; very low quality. — [EMJ BET 2017 (PubMed 28539371)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28539371/)
- **Rhythm and thoracostomy:** Stifkens et al., J Emerg Med 2019, "Prehospital Simple Thoracostomy for Traumatic Cardiac Arrest: Does the Cardiac Arrest Rhythm Matter?" Abstract not retrieved. — [PubMed 30979403](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30979403/)

### Inferences
- **Decision point:** tamponade on POCUS in an arrested or peri-arrest trauma patient → **thoracotomy/pericardiotomy by the most capable operator immediately**.
  - Needle or catheter pericardiocentesis is reasonable only as a bridge when no one can perform a thoracotomy, or in a non-arrested patient awaiting the OR. Clotted hemopericardium often defeats aspiration; that is a widely held clinical rationale, but I did not find a source for it in this search.
  - The 15-minute window in London data argues for "no delay".
- **Bilateral thoracostomy** (finger/simple in a ventilated patient; tube thoracostomy in the ED) is low-risk. It is recommended in the initial TCA bundle, and in non-ERT candidates should be done before declaring death (UAB: "both pleural spaces decompressed … before cessation").

### Gaps
- I found no comparative study of pericardiocentesis vs thoracotomy specifically in traumatic arrest. The available data are mixed-population or pre-2015.
- The exact ERC 2021 and 2025 wording and grading for pericardiocentesis and thoracostomy could not be verified (full text inaccessible).

## 6. ECPR/ECMO in traumatic cardiac arrest

### Takeaway
Most ECPR programs treat TCA as a contraindication because of bleeding and flow concerns. The only dedicated systematic review (2025) found 84 patients in 9 low-quality studies, with 39% survival to discharge, almost all with good neurologic recovery. Survivors had chest injury and respiratory-cause TCA, shorter CPR before ECPR, and surgery after cannulation; no TBI patient survived. Most cases used heparin-free or delayed-anticoagulation circuits. ECPR in trauma is hypothesis-generating; the strongest niche is hypothermic arrest with trauma, where case reports challenge "trauma = absolute contraindication".

### Cited Findings
- **Dedicated systematic review/meta-analysis [2025]:** Erblich R, Swol J, Brohi K, Dünser MW, et al. — [Scand J Trauma Resusc Emerg Med 2025 (PubMed 41449475; PMC12849657)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12849657/)
  - Studies: 9 (4 retrospective, 2 case series, 3 case reports); 84 TCA patients; all ECPR in-hospital; 54% blunt, 46% penetrating.
  - Survival: "Thirty-three (39%) patients survived to hospital discharge. All survivors recovered completely or with minor to moderate neurological disability."
  - Survivors vs non-survivors:
    - TCA due to respiratory failure: 56% vs 18%.
    - Out-of-hospital TCA: 36% vs 14%.
    - CPR before ECPR: 43 vs 78 min.
    - Surgery after ECPR: 100% vs 0%.
    - "While all survivors sustained chest injuries, no patient with traumatic brain injuries survived."
  - Feasibility: one study could not maintain adequate flow in 4/11 (36%). Those patients had higher ISS (65 vs 46.5), longer active bleeding (385 vs 201 min) and longer arrest before ECPR (79 vs 56 min).
  - Anticoagulation: "21 patients (74%) received no initial anticoagulation". One series used heparin-free circuits, with "Heparin administration delayed until surgical haemostasis and absence of active bleeding (16.7 ± 19 h after ECPR)", then UFH to "aPTT of 40–50 s or ACT of 160–180 s".
  - Complications: 56% had ≥1 (circuit 25%, neurological 22%, renal, haemorrhagic and cardiovascular 16% each).
  - Field stance: "Many ECPR programs, however, consider TCA a contraindication to ECPR because of the unknown feasibility to establish adequate extracorporeal blood flow and bleeding risks."
  - Authors' conclusion: results "are hypothesis-generating and suggest that ECPR may be feasible and potentially beneficial in selected patients with TCA."
- **Hypothermia + trauma:**
  - Mendonca et al., "Extracorporeal life support in hypothermic cardiac arrest: Reconsidering trauma as an absolute contraindication" (Resuscitation 2019). Title only; abstract not retrieved. — [PubMed 30562595](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30562595/)
  - Two Japanese case reports of hypothermic arrest (25.0 °C and 17.4 °C) with trauma: CT before ECPR to exclude major hemorrhage, both with full neurologic recovery. Case reports only. — [Wada et al., Cureus 2024 (PMC11386295)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11386295/)
- **General (non-trauma) ECPR principle:** in prospective studies, "arrest to ECMO flow time was predictive of survival", and more selection criteria were associated with better outcomes. — [Karve et al., Scand J Trauma Resusc Emerg Med 2021 (PMC8474891)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8474891/)

### Inferences
- **Typical candidate profiles** suggested by the data (low-certainty inference):
  - Hypothermic arrest with minor trauma.
  - Respiratory or chest-injury-driven arrest (e.g., massive pulmonary contusion or airway injury) where bleeding is controllable.
  - Arrest after hemorrhage control in the OR or hybrid ER.
- **Practical contraindications:** severe TBI; uncontrolled exsanguination with high ISS; prolonged low-flow time.
- Consistent with the data, ECPR in trauma would usually follow, not replace, surgical or endovascular hemostasis. A hybrid ER that can do CT, hemostasis and cannulation in one room is the setting where it is most plausible. That is an inference, not tested evidence.
- Who decides: the trauma surgeon, the ECMO team and the ED physician jointly. No guideline specifies this for trauma.

### Gaps
- I found no society guideline (ELSO, EAST, WTA, ERC) with trauma-specific ECPR criteria in the accessible sources. ERC 2025 hypothermia/ECLS text was not accessible.
- I found no Japanese registry (JAAM-OHCA or JTDB) ECPR-in-trauma study in this search, though such data may exist.

## 7. Expected survival and neurologic outcomes in TCA, overall and by mechanism/procedure

### Takeaway
- Overall, about 1 in 20 prehospital TCA patients survive (mortality 96.2%), and about 40% of survivors have a favorable neurologic outcome.
- Contemporary registry survival is 1.9–2.8%.
- After EDT, survival is about 21% for penetrating thoracic with SOL, falling to 0.7% for blunt without SOL.
- After prehospital RT, survival is 21% for tamponade vs 1.9% for exsanguination.
- After REBOA in arrest, mortality is about 88–90%.
- After ECPR (highly selected), survival is 39%.
- Survivors who reach discharge after RT, REBOA or ECPR are mostly neurologically intact.

### Cited Findings
- **Prehospital TCA meta-analysis [2022]** (36 studies, 51,722 patients) — [Vianen et al., Eur J Trauma Emerg Surg 2022 (PMC9360068)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9360068/):
  - "Overall mortality for TCA was 96.2% and a favorable neurological outcome was seen in 43.5% of the survivors."
  - Mortality was 97.6% without vs 93.9% with a physician on scene.
  - "Approximately 1 in 20 patients with prehospital TCA will survive; about 40% of survivors have favorable neurological outcome."
- **Population registry, Nova Scotia 2007–2023 [2026]:** 1,042 adults, 71.7% blunt. Survival to discharge was 1.9%. Survival was associated with critical care transport (OR 27.16) and direct transport (OR 4.10). — [Green et al., CJEM 2026 (PubMed 42507326)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42507326/)
- **EDT by mechanism and SOL (EAST 2015 pooled):** see Section 1. Penetrating thoracic with SOL 21.3% (neurologically intact 11.7%) down to blunt without SOL 0.7% (0.1%). — [PubMed 26091330](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26091330/)
- **WTA 2012 summary:** about 35% for penetrating cardiac wound in shock; 15% for all penetrating; blunt 2% (shock) and <1% (no vital signs). — [PubMed 23188227](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23188227/)
- **Contemporary EDT series:**
  - Prospective 2-year urban series, 215 EDTs (78% penetrating), mean CPR 12 min: overall survival 6/215 (2.8%); penetrating cardiac injury 6/74 (8.2%). — [Asensio et al., Surgery 2026 (PubMed 42833938)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42833938/)
  - Prospective 16-center EAST-affiliated study (2015–2016): 305 EDTs (68% GSW; 57% prehospital CPR; 37% ED SOL), "15 patients survived (13 neurologically intact)", about 4.9%. The occupational exposure rate was 7.2% per EDT, mostly trainee percutaneous injuries; each PPE item reduced exposure risk (OR 0.66). — [Nunn et al., J Trauma Acute Care Surg 2018 (PubMed 29664893)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29664893/)
  - Prospective RT + FAST series: 3.2% survival (+1.6% organ donation). — [Inaba 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26258320/)
- **Japan, JTDB 2019–2021 [2023]** (1,289 adults in arrest at scene and on arrival; 374 EDT) — [Yamamoto et al., Sci Rep 2023 (PMC10520031)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10520031/):
  - "EDT was associated with lower survival to discharge (4/374 [1.1%] vs. 22/915 [2.4%]; adjusted odds ratio [OR], 0.43 [95% CI 0.22-0.84])."
  - There was no association in severe chest injury (1.0% vs 1.4%; aOR 0.72, 0.28–1.84).
  - "The longest transportation time for survivors was 8 and 23 min in patients with and without EDT, respectively."
  - Authors: "The idea that EDT is the last resort for t-OHCA should be reconsidered".
  - Observational with IPW; residual confounding likely.
- **Prehospital RT, London [2025]:** 5.0% overall survival; 76.6% of survivors neurologically favorable. Tamponade 21%, exsanguination 1.9%, combined 0%. Cause of TCA (aOR 21.1) and duration of TCA (aOR 20.9) were independently associated with survival. — [Perkins et al., JAMA Surg 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11866073/)
- **REBOA in arrest:**
  - 90% 30-day mortality (Maryland). — [Brenner 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28973104/)
  - 88% vs 93% mortality, REBOA vs RT. — [Koh 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37381147/)
- **ECPR in TCA:** 39% survival to discharge in 84 highly selected, publication-biased cases. — [Erblich 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12849657/)
- **Blunt-specific RT:** a German level I center, 15 blunt TCA patients with RT: 24-h survival 20%, overall survival 7%. Taken from search summary; small series. — [PMC10519862](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10519862/)

### Inferences
- Outcome is driven chiefly by **cause** (tamponade and isolated penetrating cardiac injury are best; blunt exsanguination is worst), **time** (minutes of CPR or arrest before intervention), and **SOL/cardiac motion**, more than by which invasive tool is used.
- Survival figures differ by denominator: prehospital-inclusive vs ED-arrival populations, and those selected for EDT vs all-comers. Quote the denominator when comparing.
- The Japanese JTDB data (mostly blunt, persistent arrest) suggest that routine EDT in blunt TCA without a thoracic indication may not help and may harm. This aligns with EAST's conditional-against and WTA's time gates.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a 2018–2026 meta-analysis pooling EDT survival specifically. EAST 2015 remains the largest pooled EDT dataset.
- The pooled estimates of the Tran 2020 prognostic meta-analysis were not verified from the primary source.
- Neurologic outcome reporting is inconsistent across studies (CPC, GOS, or "neurologically intact" undefined).
